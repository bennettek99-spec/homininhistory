Lucy: The 3.2-Million-Year-Old Skeleton That Changed Everything

A 40% complete skeleton, barely a metre tall, with a chimp-sized brain and a hip built for walking. Lucy settled one of the oldest arguments in human origins.

Species Profile10 min readPublished August 24, 2026
The short answer

Lucy is a 3.2-million-year-old Australopithecus afarensis skeleton found at Hadar, Ethiopia in 1974. About 1.05 m tall with a brain of roughly 400 cc, she walked upright on a modern-looking pelvis and knee. She proved bipedalism evolved long before big brains — overturning a century of assumptions.

On the morning of 24 November 1974, Donald Johanson and graduate student Tom Gray were driving back from a fruitless survey in the Afar badlands of Ethiopia when Johanson glanced into a gully and saw a scrap of arm bone. By the end of the season the team had recovered 47 bones from a single individual — roughly 40% of a complete skeleton, an unheard-of haul for a fossil more than three million years old. That night, with a Beatles tape playing in camp, they named her Lucy.

Ethiopians know her by a better name: Dinkinesh, Amharic for “you are marvellous.” Half a century on, Lucy remains the single most important fossil in the study of human origins — not because she is the oldest, but because she is the most complete early hominin we have, and because her skeleton answered a question that had been argued about since Darwin.

Why Lucy mattered so much

For most of the twentieth century, scientists assumed the human story began with the brain. The reasoning felt obvious: intelligence made us tool-users, tools freed the hands, and freed hands made upright walking useful. The infamous Piltdown forgery — a large modern braincase paired with an ape’s jaw — succeeded for forty years partly because it told people exactly the story they expected.

Lucy demolished it. Her braincase was small, roughly 400 cubic centimetres, squarely inside the chimpanzee range. Her pelvis, however, was short, broad and bowl-shaped like ours, and her femur angled inward from hip to knee — the valgus angle that lets a biped balance over one foot at a time. Her knee joint has the flared, load-bearing surfaces of a habitual walker. She was, unambiguously, walking upright 3.2 million years ago with a brain no bigger than an ape’s.

The sequence of human evolution flipped. Upright walking came first, by millions of years; the brain came later. Almost everything we now ask about why our ancestors stood up is downstream of that single skeleton.

What Lucy looked like

She was tiny. At about 1.05 metres and 28 kilograms, Lucy would come up to the chest of an average adult today. Her face projected forward (prognathism), she had no chin, and her canine teeth were reduced compared with an ape’s but still larger than ours. Her rib cage was cone-shaped, flaring outward toward the bottom to house a large, plant-processing gut.

Her arms tell a more complicated story than her legs. Lucy’s finger and toe bones are curved, her arms are long relative to her legs, and her shoulder sockets angle upward — features associated with climbing. Researchers still argue about how much of this is a genuine arboreal adaptation and how much is inherited baggage that no longer mattered. The most common reading today: A. afarensis walked competently on the ground and still slept and fed in trees, which is not a contradiction so much as a sensible strategy for a small animal in a landscape full of leopards and giant hyenas.

Her species: Australopithecus afarensis

Lucy is the best-known member of Australopithecus afarensis, a species that lived in eastern Africa from roughly 3.9 to 2.9 million years ago — a run of about a million years, several times longer than our own species has existed so far. Other landmark finds fill out the picture:

  • The First Family (AL 333) — at least 13 individuals of mixed ages found together at Hadar, hinting at group living.
  • Selam (DIK-1-1), sometimes called “Lucy’s baby” — a three-year-old girl from Dikika, Ethiopia, dated to 3.3 million years and described by Zeresenay Alemseged in 2006. She is geologically older than Lucy but younger at death, and preserves a hyoid bone and a near-complete skull.
  • Kadanuumuu (KSD-VP-1/1) — a 3.6-million-year-old partial skeleton from Woranso-Mille, much larger than Lucy, showing that the species was strongly size-dimorphic.
  • The Laetoli footprints — a trail of prints pressed into wet volcanic ash in Tanzania 3.66 million years ago, found by Mary Leakey’s team in 1976. They show a striding, heel-first gait with a non-grasping big toe. Nothing else in the record makes bipedalism this vivid.

How did Lucy die?

For decades, nobody knew. Then in 2016 a team led by John Kappelman published a CT-based study in Nature arguing that the pattern of fractures in her right shoulder, arm, ankle and knee looked like the compression injuries a modern trauma surgeon sees after a fall from height — perhaps from a tree, arms outstretched.

The claim is contested. Johanson himself and several Ethiopian researchers argue the cracks are ordinary fossilisation damage of the kind visible on countless bones from the same deposits. It is an honest, unresolved argument, and a useful reminder that a fossil this famous is still generating new questions.

Where Lucy sits in the family tree

A. afarensis is widely treated as a plausible ancestor of later hominins, including the lineage that produced Homo habilis and eventually us — but the branch points are genuinely murky. The species overlaps in time with Kenyanthropus platyops and Australopithecus deyiremeda, and the 3.4-million-year-old Burtele foot from Ethiopia shows a second, more grasping-footed hominin sharing the same landscape. Middle Pliocene Africa held more than one kind of upright ape.

What is not in doubt is Lucy’s position as a benchmark. Every new early hominin gets measured against her.

Why she still matters

Lucy did more than settle the brain-versus-bipedalism question. She made human evolution legible to the public — a named individual with a body, a size and a way of moving, rather than a drawer of fragments. She also set the standard for what a good early hominin fossil can tell us: not just what a species looked like, but how it moved, what it ate, how it grew and how it died.

And she is a reminder of how thin the record is. Lucy is 3.2 million years old and 40% complete. Nearly everything else from that depth of time is a tooth, a jaw fragment, or a single bone.

Frequently asked questions

How old is the Lucy fossil?

Lucy is dated to about 3.18 million years old, based on argon-argon dating of the volcanic ash layers above and below the sediments at Hadar, Ethiopia, where she was found.

What species was Lucy?

Lucy belongs to Australopithecus afarensis, a species that lived in eastern Africa between roughly 3.9 and 2.9 million years ago. She is its most complete known individual.

How tall was Lucy?

About 1.05 metres (3 feet 6 inches) tall and around 28 kilograms (62 pounds). Male A. afarensis were substantially larger.

Why is Lucy so important?

She proved that upright walking evolved long before large brains. Lucy had a bowl-shaped pelvis and an inward-angled femur built for bipedalism, but a brain of only about 400 cc — the size of a chimpanzee’s.

Where is Lucy kept today?

The original fossil is held at the National Museum of Ethiopia in Addis Ababa. Museums elsewhere display casts; the original toured the United States between 2007 and 2013.
