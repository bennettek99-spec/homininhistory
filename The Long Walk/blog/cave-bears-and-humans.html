Cave Bears and Humans: A 400,000-Year Relationship

The giant cave bear shared Europe's caverns with Neanderthals and modern humans, inspiring hunts, artwork, and one of archaeology's most stubborn myths.

Prehistoric Fauna10 min readPublished July 15, 2026
The short answer

The giant cave bear shared Europe’s caverns with Neanderthals and modern humans for 400,000 years — inspiring hunts, cave art, and one of archaeology’s most persistent myths, the ‘cave bear cult’.

Few Ice Age animals loomed larger in the human imagination than Ursus spelaeus, the cave bear. Bigger than a modern grizzly and named for the caves where its bones pile up by the thousand, it was both a rival for shelter and, eventually, a subject of art.

Meet the cave bear

Cave bear males could weigh close to a tonne, dwarfing today’s brown bears. Despite their size and huge canines, wear on their broad molars shows they were largely vegetarian, fuelling up before hibernating deep in caves — where many died in their sleep during hard winters, leaving the vast bone deposits that give the species its name.

Sharing the cave

For Neanderthals and later modern humans, caves were prime real estate — and so they were for bears. People and bears used the same shelters, generally at different seasons, and the archaeology preserves an uneasy, 400,000-year cohabitation.

The 'cave bear cult' myth

In the 1920s, excavations at Drachenloch in the Swiss Alps reported bear skulls arranged in stone ‘altars’, taken as evidence of a Neanderthal cave bear cult. The idea gripped the public imagination for decades. Careful re-analysis, though, showed the arrangements were almost certainly natural — bones shifted by water, sediment and the bears themselves. It is a classic case of a vivid story outrunning the evidence.

Bears in Ice Age art

What is not in doubt is that bears mattered to Ice Age people. At Chauvet Cave in France, home to more than 12,000 cave bear bones, a single bear skull sits on a fallen slab of rock, and bears feature among the paintings. At Montespan, a headless clay bear was modelled on the cave floor. Whatever the meaning, the animal was clearly on the artists’ minds.

Hunting and extinction

Humans did hunt cave bears, though how often is debated. The species vanished around 24,000 years ago. Ancient DNA points to a long, slow decline: low genetic diversity, a cooling climate that shrank their plant food, and mounting pressure from expanding human populations competing for the same caves.

Why it matters

The cave bear is a window onto the deep entanglement of humans and the animals we lived beside — and a caution about reading too much ritual into the fossil record, a debate that echoes in claims about ancient burial today.

Frequently asked questions

Did humans worship cave bears?

Probably not. The famous ‘cave bear cult’ based on skull arrangements at Drachenloch has been largely debunked as a natural accumulation, though cave bears clearly featured in Ice Age art and imagination.

Did Neanderthals live in caves with bears?

They used the same caves, generally at different times or seasons. Humans and cave bears shared Europe’s caverns for hundreds of thousands of years in an uneasy competition for shelter.

Why did cave bears go extinct?

Ancient DNA suggests a long decline driven by low genetic diversity, a cooling climate that reduced their plant food, and growing competition with humans for caves. They disappeared around 24,000 years ago.
